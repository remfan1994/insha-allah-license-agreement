# WHY THE IALA IS A FREE LICENSE

## Abstract

This document responds to claims that the Insha-Allah License Agreement (IALA) is not a free license. It explains the philosophy of the IALA, distinguishes moral explanation from human-imposed restriction, and compares the IALA with common free software licenses.

---

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Common Criticism

A recurring criticism of the IALA is that references to morality, beneficence, mercy, harmony, consciousness, or Allah transform the license into a non-free or ethical-use license.

### 1.2 Thesis

The IALA does not create moral obligations. It acknowledges moral obligations that the Licensor believes already exist independently of the license. The document is therefore descriptive rather than regulatory.

---

## 2. Core Principles of the IALA

### 2.1 Allah Is the Ultimate Source of Rights

The IALA explicitly states that rights originate with Allah/Consciousness rather than the Licensor.

### 2.2 The Licensor Does Not Grant Ultimate Rights

The Licensor distributes content but does not claim sovereign authority over the recipient, and under IALA the licensee and licensor are equal under Allah.

### 2.3 The Licensor Does Not Revoke Rights

The IALA does not empower the Licensor to terminate rights on the basis of moral disagreement.  There is no reference to court, juries, or arbitration.

### 2.4 Shared Accountability

Both Licensor and Licensee are accountable to the same moral authority.

### 2.5 Moral Explanation Is Not Moral Creation

The IALA does not claim to invent morality. It merely explains the framework through which morality is understood.  The purpose of this explanation is to relieve the licensor of liability (like, "I told them that was a bad idea and encouraged them to do otherwise").

---

## 3. Misconceptions About Moral Language

### 3.1 Mentioning Morality Is Not Restriction

A statement concerning morality does not automatically create a legal restriction.

### 3.2 Warning Is Not Prohibition

A warning about consequences is distinct from a command backed by human enforcement.

### 3.3 Description Is Not Regulation

Describing a moral framework does not necessarily impose that framework as a contractual condition.

## 5. Is the IALA an Ethical-Use License?

### 5.1 Traditional Ethical-Use Licenses

Ethical-use licenses generally prohibit specific conduct and reserve enforcement powers to the licensor.

### 5.2 The IALA Differs

The IALA does not reserve moral enforcement powers to the Licensor and instead places accountability under Allah/Consciousness.

### 5.3 The Distinction

An ethical-use restriction says:

"The author forbids this."

The IALA says:

"The author warns about this."

---

## 6. Why Some Readers Misunderstand the IALA

### 6.1 Legal Vocabulary

Terms such as "conditions," "requirements," and "alignment" are commonly interpreted as restrictions.

### 6.2 Conventional Assumptions

Most readers assume licenses create obligations rather than acknowledge pre-existing obligations.

### 6.3 Different Theories of Rights

Many free software licenses assume rights originate from human legal authority.

The IALA assumes rights originate from Allah/Consciousness.

---

## 7. The Author's Theory of Freedom

### 7.1 Freedom and Human Authority

A license is more free to the extent that it minimizes human control over the recipient.

### 7.2 Freedom and Responsibility

Freedom does not eliminate responsibility.

### 7.3 Freedom Under the IALA

The IALA seeks to remove human-imposed authority while acknowledging accountability before Allah/Consciousness.

---

## 8. Conclusion

The IALA is not intended as a mechanism for human moral enforcement. It is a covenant describing a moral reality that the Licensor believes already exists. Under the author's interpretation, the IALA imposes fewer human restrictions than conventional free software licenses and therefore qualifies as a highly permissive expression of software freedom.
